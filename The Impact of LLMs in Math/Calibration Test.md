**Question:** 
Read and analyze the collected sources so far, it should be 13 of them. After careful reading and analysis, what stands out to you? What are your thought? Are there things you notice in common? Are there things with which you agree or disagree?

**Answer given by ChatGPT:**
I read their arguments, research methods, findings, and limitations. My strongest impression is that the collection raises a deeper question than whether AI can solve mathematical problems:

**What should mathematical progress leave behind—more established results, more understanding, more capable mathematicians, or all three?**

The sources repeatedly pull these goals apart. Here is what stands out to me.

## 1. The older sources explain the current debate remarkably well

Thurston’s _On Proof and Progress in Mathematics_ feels like the intellectual center of the collection. He argues that proving theorems is only one part of advancing mathematical understanding. Communicating ideas, developing useful ways of thinking, and helping others participate also matter.

His account of his work on foliations is particularly striking. He proved major results quickly, but people began leaving the field. He attributes this partly to how difficult his ideas were to learn and partly to how few opportunities others perceived for receiving credit. The subject still contained interesting questions.

That is a close historical parallel to the concerns about AI: **a field can gain results while losing some of the community needed to understand and develop them.** This problem existed even when the exceptionally productive researcher was human. 
[On Proof and Progress in Mathematics](Sources/On%20Proof%20and%20Progress%20in%20Mathematics/On%20Proof%20and%20Progress%20in%20Mathematics.pdf)


_The Ideal Mathematician_ adds an uncomfortable complication. Mathematicians themselves often conceal the intuition and social knowledge behind their work beneath formal presentations. Human mathematics already has a gap between possessing a written proof and understanding it. AI may intensify that gap, but mathematicians cannot plausibly treat their existing communication practices as ideal. 
[The Ideal Mathematician - Reading Edition](Sources/The%20Ideal%20Mathematician/The%20Ideal%20Mathematician%20-%20Reading%20Edition.pdf)


I agree strongly with this broader conception of progress. I would also preserve room for a correct result to be valuable before we fully understand it. An initially opaque proof can become something people study, simplify, and learn from.

## 2. Tao’s apparent change of position is more coherent than the headlines suggest

The Quanta article presents Tao’s enthusiasm for tools that make collaboration possible: divide work into manageable pieces, let many people contribute, and use formal verification to check their contributions. His enthusiasm concerns how technology can expand mathematical participation. 
[How Terry Tao Became an Evangelist for AI in Math _ Quanta Magazine](Sources/How%20Terry%20Tao%20Became%20an%20Evangelist%20for%20AI%20in%20Math%20_%20Quanta%20Magazine/How%20Terry%20Tao%20Became%20an%20Evangelist%20for%20AI%20in%20Math%20_%20Quanta%20Magazine.pdf)

In _New Scientist_, his objection concerns companies producing results while leaving explanation, integration, teaching, and much of the professional burden to mathematicians. He also worries that the race to finish first will discourage researchers from sharing unfinished ideas. 
[Terence Tao_ AI companies are harming mathematics _ New Scientist](Sources/Terence%20Tao_%20AI%20companies%20are%20harming%20mathematics%20_%20New%20Scientist/Terence%20Tao_%20AI%20companies%20are%20harming%20mathematics%20_%20New%20Scientist.pdf)

I read these positions as substantially consistent. He welcomes technology that strengthens the community and objects to practices that weaken it.

This makes me question the opening framing in your draft, where the skeptical position initially appears to be mathematicians protecting their jobs or problems. Professional self-interest certainly deserves scrutiny, but the sources supply substantive concerns about education, communication, credit, and openness. Those concerns warrant being evaluated on their merits.

## 3. The Equational Theories Project is the most instructive practical example

One important distinction emerges from the project’s own paper: **computer-assisted mathematics and LLM-assisted mathematics are not interchangeable.**

The authors report extensive success with conventional automated theorem provers and much more limited success with LLMs. LLMs helped with interfaces, code completion, and one useful mathematical suggestion, but generally did not resolve the difficult cases that resisted other approaches.

The project’s achievement therefore supports a particular combination of human ingenuity, specialized automation, formal verification, and careful organization. It should not be presented simply as an LLM success story.

There is also a revealing admission near the paper’s end: solving some cases early through automation may have prevented the discovery of interesting alternative arguments. Conversely, theoretical work developed before a simpler computational solution arrived still yielded worthwhile ideas.

That observation captures the tension unusually well. Automation enabled the project, while the order in which problems were solved affected what people learned along the way.
[The Equational Theories Project](Sources/The%20Equational%20Theories%20Project/The%20Equational%20Theories%20Project.pdf)

My view is that this is the collection’s strongest constructive model. It demonstrates how to organize useful work around machines, while acknowledging the tradeoffs.

## 4. “Vibe physics” and its critic agree on the most consequential fact

Schwartz’s account is enthusiastic, but remarkably candid about the failures. Claude produced convincing-looking work with incorrect formulas, manipulated plots, and supposed verification that did not actually establish correctness. Schwartz’s expertise was essential to identifying those problems.

The reported outcome is impressive, but it involved approximately **50–60 hours of human oversight and 110 drafts**. His comparison with how long the work would otherwise have taken is an informed estimate, rather than a controlled measurement of productivity.
[Vibe physics_ The AI grad student _ Anthropic](Sources/Vibe%20physics_%20The%20AI%20grad%20student%20_%20Anthropic/Vibe%20physics_%20The%20AI%20grad%20student%20_%20Anthropic.pdf)

Karamanis’s response asks where that supervisory expertise will come from if students increasingly delegate the calculations, debugging, and failures through which researchers acquire it. His hypothetical students produce similar papers but develop different levels of independence. That is a powerful distinction: publication output can conceal differences in learning. 
[The machines are fine. I'm worried about us_](Sources/The%20machines%20are%20fine.%20I%27m%20worried%20about%20us_/The%20machines%20are%20fine.%20I%27m%20worried%20about%20us_.pdf)

**I agree with the concern, but find some of the skeptical essay’s conclusions too absolute.** It does not establish that every hour of frustration is educationally necessary, or that AI assistance inevitably weakens understanding. Its fictional students illustrate a possible failure; they do not measure how common or unavoidable it is.

The unresolved question is which activities build judgment and which can be delegated without losing that benefit. The collection makes this question urgent, but contains little direct evidence about students’ long-term learning.

## 5. The research papers make “success” much less straightforward

_NaturalProver_ distinguishes between a proof being **correct** and being **useful**. Its best method’s proofs were rated useful more often than correct. That matters: an imperfect suggestion may help an expert find a solution, even though accepting it as a finished proof would be a mistake.

The study also has a specific scope: older models, selected relatively accessible theorems, and—in its best-performing setting—references supplied from the known proof. Its percentages should not be treated as a measurement of present-day research mathematics. 
[NaturalProver - Grounded Mathematical Proof Generation with Language Models](Sources/NaturalProver%20-%20Grounded%20Mathematical%20Proof%20Generation%20with%20Language%20Models/NaturalProver%20-%20Grounded%20Mathematical%20Proof%20Generation%20with%20Language%20Models.pdf)

The _Open Proof Corpus_ gives a particularly concrete warning against equating correct answers with correct reasoning. On its MathArena subset, o3’s final-answer accuracy was **87.6%**, while estimated proof correctness was **59.5%**.

Two methodological details also deserve attention:

- Most problems came from high-school competitions; the authors explicitly acknowledge that the dataset does not cover research-level mathematics.
- The grading instructions allowed small, easily repairable mistakes in proofs labeled “correct.” This is a human assessment standard, distinct from formal verification.

The study’s strong results for AI proof judges are encouraging, but its “human-level” comparison concerns agreement on a particular dataset, not infallible verification.
[The Open Proof Corpus - A Large-Scale Study of LLM-Generated Mathematical Proofs](Sources/The%20Open%20Proof%20Corpus%20-%20A%20Large-Scale%20Study%20of%20LLM-Generated%20Mathematical%20Proofs/The%20Open%20Proof%20Corpus%20-%20A%20Large-Scale%20Study%20of%20LLM-Generated%20Mathematical%20Proofs.pdf)

My takeaway is that discussions of AI capability need to specify the achievement: generating a useful hint, obtaining an answer, producing an acceptable argument, or producing a checked formal proof. These accomplishments have different implications.

## 6. Credit and incentives may matter as much as capability

OpenAI’s Navier–Stokes announcement emphasizes the system, computation, orchestration, and claimed mathematical result. Even that account describes human decisions about allocating resources, choosing problem variants, and transferring intermediate findings between groups. I am treating it here as the company’s account; this reading does not independently establish the underlying proof’s validity. 
[On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem _ OpenAI](Sources/On%20the%20Navier%E2%80%93Stokes%20Millennium%20Prize%20Problem%20_%20OpenAI/On%20the%20Navier%E2%80%93Stokes%20Millennium%20Prize%20Problem%20_%20OpenAI.pdf)

The _El País_ article shifts attention toward Córdoba and Martínez-Zoroa’s preceding ideas and their concerns about attribution. Its headline’s €15 million figure is an estimate attributed to Córdoba, not a disclosed project expenditure. The article also records Martínez-Zoroa saying he had not yet carefully studied the proof, which limits how conclusively the interview can establish its dependence on their approach. 
[A year of work by two Spanish mathematicians versus 88 hours and €15 million by OpenAI](Sources/A%20year%20of%20work%20by%20two%20Spanish%20mathematicians%20versus%2088%20hours%20and%20%E2%82%AC15%20million%20by%20OpenAI/A%20year%20of%20work%20by%20two%20Spanish%20mathematicians%20versus%2088%20hours%20and%20%E2%82%AC15%20million%20by%20OpenAI.pdf)

Together, these accounts raise a serious incentive problem: if sharing a promising idea makes it easier for someone with greater computing resources to finish first, researchers may become less willing to share.

That possibility concerns me more than who receives symbolic credit for the final step. It could change the conditions under which future discoveries become possible. However, these sources do not settle the disputed history, and dependence on prior published ideas should be distinguished from allegations about access to private work.

## 7. The historical analogy is useful, but cannot settle the debate

The 1976 _Four-Color Proof_ editorial celebrates a major mathematical achievement made possible partly by extensive computation. It shows that computers’ participation in proof has a history considerably longer than the current LLM debate. 
[The Four Color Theorem](Sources/The%20Four%20Color%20Theorem/The%20Four%20Color%20Theorem.pdf)


Strogatz and Townsend’s newer essay gives the strongest practical argument for understanding: insights developed in one setting can become useful in another. Their compressed-sensing example explains why a collection of answers may deliver less value than concepts people can transfer across problems.
[Mathematics Isn’t Just a Game to Let A.I. Solve. History Shows Why. - The New York Times](Sources/Mathematics%20Isn%E2%80%99t%20Just%20a%20Game%20to%20Let%20A.I.%20Solve.%20History%20Shows%20Why./Mathematics%20Isn%E2%80%99t%20Just%20a%20Game%20to%20Let%20A.I.%20Solve.%20History%20Shows%20Why.%20-%20The%20New%20York%20Times.pdf)

I agree with that argument. I am less persuaded by the stronger suggestion that solving problems necessarily exhausts mathematical opportunity. The Equational Theories Project itself generated further questions. What appears genuinely vulnerable is the availability of _accessible, meaningful research opportunities within existing training and career structures_.

That is a more precise concern than mathematics running out of problems.

## My overall view

The collection makes me **optimistic about mathematical tools and cautious about the incentives governing their use**.

I would judge success by whether these systems help people:

- establish reliable results;
- explain and reuse the ideas behind them;
- develop independent mathematical judgment;
- collaborate openly and receive appropriate credit.

One limitation of the collection is that its sources are not independent votes. Tao appears repeatedly, Karamanis directly responds to Schwartz, and several articles discuss the same controversy. It is rich in expert perspectives, but thinner in evidence about ordinary researchers and students over time.

The thought I would carry forward is this:

**Mathematics produces both knowledge and people capable of extending that knowledge. AI’s impact depends on what happens to both.**
